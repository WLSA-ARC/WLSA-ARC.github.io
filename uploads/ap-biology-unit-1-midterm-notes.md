# AP Biology Unit 1 Review Notes

## Chemical bonding & Electronegativity

- Electronegativity: Ability to attract outer‑shell electrons. Fluorine has highest electronegativity; Francium lowest.

- N, H, group‑18 elements have special electronegativity values that need to be memorized.

## Water

- Hydrogen bond: intermolecular attraction.

- Requirement for hydrogen bond: must form between H and O / N / F.

- Hydrogen bonds are sensitive, not stable and can be broken.

- One water molecule can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds.

- Low temperature → more hydrogen bonds form → water becomes ice.

- Water vapor: no hydrogen bonds present (temperature is too high).

## Characteristic of water

1. Cohesion: Attraction between water molecules caused by hydrogen bonds.

- Cohesion gives water high boiling point (hydrogen bonds require energy to break).

- Produces convex water surface.

2. Adhesion: attraction between water molecule and other non‑water substances.

3. Thermal property: high specific heat capacity; resists temperature change; keeps conditions stable.

4. Solvent property: good solvent. Lipids / oils cannot dissolve in water (non‑polar substances).

Polar substances are soluble in water.

## Organic substance

Two criteria for organic substances：

1. Contains carbon and hydrogen

2. C‑H bonds are covalent bonds

## Functional Groups

1. Hydroxyl (-OH): polar functional group

2. Carboxyl (-COOH): releases H+， acidic

3. Amino (-NH₂): accepts H+, basic

4. Phosphate group: acidic, carries negative charge

5. Methyl (-CH₃): non‑polar; C and H have similar electronegativity.

## Metabolism

- Metabolic chemical reactions are enzyme‑catalyzed.

- Definition notes：

- in‑vivo: reactions that take place inside living things (where metabolism occurs)

- in‑vitro: term for reactions occurring outside living things (not metabolism)

1. Anabolism: build small molecules → large molecules.

2. Catabolism: break down large molecules → small molecules.

## Condensation / Dehydration reaction & Hydrolysis

- Condensation reaction: Two small molecules combine to form one larger molecule, losing a small molecule (e.g. $\ce{H2O}$). A new covalent bond is formed.

- Dehydration reaction: A subset of condensation reactions where water is the small molecule product.

- Hydrolysis: larger molecules are broken apart with the addition of water.

Photosynthesis uses condensation/dehydration reaction; cellular respiration uses hydrolysis.

## Monosaccharides

- Two classes: hexose, pentose

- Example: glucose

In glucose, single C‑C bonds can rotate, side groups can swap positions.

In glucose ring structure, ring bonds cannot rotate; groups above and below ring cannot swap. Swapping those groups produces α‑D‑glucose and β‑D‑glucose.

## Disaccharides

- Sucrose, lactose

- Joined together by glycosidic linkages.

## Polysaccharides

1. Starch: energy storage carbohydrate in plants

- Amylose: α‑D‑glucose connected by 1,4 glycosidic bonds, unbranched.

- Amylopectin: α‑D‑glucose; 1,4 and 1,6 glycosidic bonds; slightly branched.

2. Glycogen: exists only in mammals; highly branched α‑D‑glucose; more 1,6 glycosidic bonds than amylopectin.

3. Cellulose: linear polymer made of β‑D‑glucose; only 1,4 glycosidic bonds.

## Lipids

General property: hydrophobic / non‑polar

1. Triglycerides: long‑term energy storage.

- Structure: glycerol + 3 fatty acid chains; held together by ester bonds.

2. Phospholipids: main component of cell membranes. Amphipathic: polar hydrophilic head, non‑polar hydrophobic tails.

3. Steroids: function as hormones.

- Cholesterol: carbon skeleton: three hexagon rings + one pentagon ring.

- Serves as precursor for other steroids: cortisol, testosterone, estrogen.

- Found in animal cell membranes; modulates membrane fluidity and stability.

- At low temperature: cholesterol reduces membrane fluidity.

## Fatty acid types

1. Saturated fatty acid: only single carbon‑carbon bonds.

2. Unsaturated‑trans: contains C=C double bond; carbon chain remains straight.

3. Unsaturated‑cis: contains C=C double bond; carbon chain bends towards the side without hydrogen atoms. Double bond itself cannot rotate freely; the whole segment rotates together.

Energy comparison: Carbohydrates are partially oxidized and yield less energy. Fats can react with more $\ce{O2}$ to release much more energy; suited for long‑term, stable energy storage.

Membrane property: More cis fatty acids → higher membrane permeability.

Small non‑polar substances can pass through cell membrane easily.

## Proteins

- Monomer: amino acid (20 standard types)

- Polymer: polypeptide

- Four categories of amino acids: polar, non‑polar, positively‑charged, negatively‑charged.

- Polypeptide synthesis: new amino acid adds its N‑terminus to the C‑terminus of existing chain; peptide bond forms.

Sources of polypeptide diversity：

1. Different types of amino acids

2. Different amino acid arrangement / sequence

3. Different polypeptide chain length

4. Different folding patterns

## Four levels of protein structure

1. Primary structure: linear amino‑acid sequence of polypeptide; held together by peptide bonds.

2. Secondary structure: folding within one polypeptide chain; stabilized only by hydrogen bonds on polypeptide backbone. Two forms: α‑helix, β‑pleated sheet.

A change in primary structure will affect tertiary structure.

3. Tertiary structure: complete 3‑D shape for a single polypeptide chain. Stabilized by:

- Hydrophobic interactions

- Disulfide bridges

- Ionic bonding

- Hydrogen bonding

4. Quaternary structure: functional protein assembled from two or more distinct polypeptide chains. Not all proteins possess quaternary structure.

Temperature / pH changes disrupt higher‑order protein structures first. Denaturation happens; primary structure remains intact.

## Enzymes

- Enzymes are proteins.

- Function: speed up chemical reactions. Activity is affected by temperature and pH.

- Factors affecting reaction rate: substrate concentration, enzyme concentration.

## Inhibition

- Competitive inhibition: inhibitor competes with substrate for the enzyme active site.

→ Increase substrate concentration can reduce the effect of competitive inhibitor.

- Non‑competitive inhibition: inhibitor binds elsewhere, alters tertiary structure of enzyme and distorts active site. Cannot be overcome by adding more substrate.

Key rule: structure determines function (including solubility).

Reaction that breaks down proteins: hydrolysis.
